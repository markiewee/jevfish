# Hotel rate ladder v2: differentiated alternatives and price-blind demand

Calibration seed, version 2. Every figure is published and cited. No figure belongs to a
real Millia, Pureloft or Lazybee property, and no guest data appears anywhere.

Version 1 of this scenario described the four competing hotels as interchangeable and
identically priced. That makes them perfect substitutes, and under perfect substitution
the correct demand curve is a knife edge: nobody books a cent above the rival price and
everybody books a cent below. Real hotel demand is measured as inelastic precisely because
hotels are not interchangeable. Version 2 puts the differentiation back in.

## The hotel

A 150-room upper-upscale city hotel in a European capital, four minutes' walk from the
main station and eight from the centre of the business district. Standard double, breakfast
included, free wifi, free cancellation until 6pm on the day of arrival, 24-hour reception.
It scores 8.4 on the major booking sites. It is in the loyalty programme of a large chain.

Baseline trading position, set to the published mean of the Cornell European sample
(Enz, Canina and van der Rest, Cornell Hospitality Report 15:2, February 2015, covering
4,120 hotels across 37 countries and 17,272 hotel-years): ADR USD 180, occupancy 63
percent. The dates are an ordinary midweek Tuesday to Thursday in shoulder season. No
city-wide conference, no festival, no public holiday.

## What else the traveller can actually book

The competitive set is four hotels, and they are not interchangeable with this one or with
each other. On these dates:

- **The station hotel**, USD 180. Two minutes closer to the station, but it faces the
  tracks and scores 7.9. Rooms are smaller. Breakfast is USD 22 extra.
- **The business-district hotel**, USD 180. Right by the offices, so fifteen minutes'
  walk further from the station and from anywhere to eat at night. Scores 8.5. Its
  standard rooms on the Tuesday are already gone; only the executive room is left.
- **The boutique**, USD 180. Scores 8.8 and everybody likes it, which is why it holds only
  40 rooms and sells out early on midweek dates. No loyalty programme, no corporate rate.
- **The chain budget hotel**, USD 180 on these dates because a small trade fair has pushed
  its rate up from its usual USD 115. No breakfast, no bar, scores 7.6.

Beyond the competitive set there are serviced apartments twenty-five minutes out by metro
at around USD 130, and hostel beds at USD 45. Both are real options for some travellers
and not for others.

Travellers have already spent time searching. Most have shortlisted two or three places
and do not want to start again. Many hold status with one chain and lose it by switching.

## The decision

One rate will be published across every channel for these dates. Five options:

- **Match**: USD 180, level with the competitive set. This is the status quo.
- **Undercut by ten**: USD 162.
- **Undercut by twenty**: USD 144.
- **Premium of ten**: USD 198.
- **Premium of twenty**: USD 216.

Nothing else changes between the five. Same room, same breakfast, same cancellation terms,
same hotel, same dates, same four rivals at USD 180.

## Who is in the market, and who can even see the rate

Segment shares follow the audited FY2024 mix of Host Hotels and Resorts across its 78
comparable upper-upscale and luxury hotels: transient 54.4 percent of roomnights, group
38.8 percent, contract 6.9 percent. That matters here, because most of the group and
contract roomnights are sold at a rate agreed months ago and are not affected at all by
what the hotel publishes this week.

**Group block attendees, a large share of the house.** They are attending a company
meeting or an association event that contracted a block of rooms at a rate agreed six to
eighteen months ago. They book into the block through a link. They never see the published
rate and it does not apply to them. Whatever the hotel does with its public rate this
week, they arrive.

**Contract guests.** Airline crew on a fixed annual contract with guaranteed availability,
and two long-stay corporate accounts. Their rate is set by contract for the year. The
published rate does not reach them either.

**Business travellers on company money.** The meeting sets the dates, not them. The
employer reimburses within a per diem or a policy cap, and several book through a
corporate tool that shows a short list of approved hotels with a cap of around USD 230.
They care about being near the meeting, a quiet room, a desk, early breakfast, and free
cancellation because meetings move. Many hold chain status and lose the night's credit by
switching. A twenty dollar difference is not their money.

**Leisure couples on their own money.** They chose the city and could have chosen another,
and could shift their dates by a week. They are comparing a shortlist on one screen with
the price next to the review score, and they pay from their own pocket. They notice twenty
dollars, but they also notice that the boutique scores 8.8 and the station hotel faces the
tracks.

**Price-led travellers.** Students, backpackers, and people who will take whatever is
cheapest and acceptable. They sort by price first. The hostel at USD 45 and the serviced
apartment at USD 130 are genuinely in their consideration set, so at these rates most of
them were never going to book an upper-upscale hotel at all.

## What the published evidence says should happen

Enz, Canina and van der Rest measured this across 17,272 hotel-years. Relative to pricing
at parity with the competitive set:

- 15 to 30 percent below: occupancy up 5.90 points, RevPAR down 16.67 percent.
- 10 to 15 percent below: occupancy up 3.20 points, RevPAR down 9.52 percent.
- 10 to 15 percent above: occupancy down 1.16 points, RevPAR up 10.92 percent.
- 15 to 30 percent above: occupancy down 3.46 points, RevPAR up 15.93 percent.

In Asia-Pacific the same discount tier produced a RevPAR gap of minus 17.3 percent. The
authors concluded that lodging demand may be inelastic in local markets and that operators
may wish to resist pressure to undercut competitors. Property-level own-price elasticity
estimates in the literature run from minus 0.13 (Canina and Carvell, 481 US urban hotels,
1989 to 2000) to minus 0.95 (Fujii et al, 1985).

## The question

For each of the five rates, how many people in this market would book this hotel for these
dates at that rate?
